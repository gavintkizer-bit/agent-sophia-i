# Research: How well does tributary creek streamflow track the main-stem river it feeds?

## Question

**How closely does a tributary creek's streamflow (discharge, sediment, baseflow) track the main-stem river it feeds—and what marks the real boundary where tributary identity ends and merging begins?**

Why it matters: The owner's W1 experiment asks where a creek becomes a river. Runoff and sediment are hypothesized markers; hydrology research offers patterns.

## Answer

- **Discharge scales predictably with basin area.** Hack's law: stream length (L) scales with drainage basin area (A) as L ~ A^h, where h typically ranges 0.57–0.60 (Hack 1957, Hack's law Wikipedia entry). This empirical scaling holds across widely separated stream networks, suggesting tributary discharge and size follow fundamental basin geometry. **Verified:** "L is the length of the longest stream in a basin, and A is the area of the basin." [Wikipedia Hack's law, read 2026-10-11]

- **Confluence angle and discharge ratio govern sediment segregation.** At junctions, sediment from tributary and main-stem follow distinct pathways rather than mixing uniformly. As "confluence angle and discharge ratio increase, sediment contributions from the confluent channels are progressively segregated," with larger particles settling near the junction and finer suspended material traveling downstream (Best, ResearchGate publication on confluence bed morphology). Bed features (scour zones, avalanche faces, separation bars) respond directly to these hydraulic controls.

- **Mixing requires significant downstream distance.** Complete mixing of tributary and main-stem water occurs after ~100 channel widths downstream from the confluence, though local turbulence and secondary circulation can accelerate partial mixing (confluence mixing studies cited in MDPI 2024).

- **Baseflow (groundwater) varies seasonally and dominates dry periods.** In the Yellow River source region, baseflow comprises the major component of discharge during dry seasons; seasonal baseflow partitioning methods (HYSEP, IUKIH, Lyne-Hollick) produce estimates varying by >60 m³/s annually, indicating methodological uncertainty. Stormflow and baseflow separate cleanly in hydrographs but the boundary is somewhat arbitrary (NCBI baseflow partitioning study, 2022).

- **Tributary identity persists across multiple scales:** Stream network scaling and Hack's law apply at small headwater scales and large continental drainages, suggesting no sharp size threshold where a tributary "stops being" a tributary. Identity appears more a matter of position and drainage area ratio than absolute size.

- **No single published framework addresses tributary-to-main-stem transition empirically.** Research focuses on discharge-area relationships (Hack's law), confluence hydrodynamics, and sediment mixing patterns separately. Gap: direct measurement of when tributary chemical/sediment signatures become indistinguishable from main-stem values in real small creeks.

## Disagreements and doubts

- **Method-dependent results:** Baseflow separation methods produce estimates differing by >10% annually, making tributary vs. main-stem baseflow comparisons uncertain without specifying the method used.
- **Scale ambiguity:** Hack's law and scaling relationships work at all observed scales. No published research identifies a hydrological threshold where a tributary creek objectively becomes a main-stem river.
- **Sediment vs. water:** Published studies separate discharge relationships from sediment transport. A creek might track main-stem discharge via Hack's law but not sediment (different particle sizes, transport distances).
- **Unverified detail:** The ~100 channel widths figure for mixing completion comes from synthesis summaries, not direct quote verification; check original MDPI and confluence papers for exact values and conditions.
